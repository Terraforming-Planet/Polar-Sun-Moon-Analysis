# Arctic 90°N Mountain-Island Hypothesis

## Status

**Open research hypothesis — not a construction authorization.**

This document records a deliberately extreme idea: investigate whether a permanent artificial mountain/island fixed at the geographic North Pole could become a uniquely stable Earth-observation reference point and whether such a structure would measurably alter local or regional ocean, sea-ice and atmosphere dynamics.

The proposal must be judged by measurement and simulation, not by assumption. If modeling shows unacceptable environmental or geophysical risk, the concept must be rejected or redesigned.

## Proposed geometry

Initial simulation envelope:

- geographic reference: **90°N**;
- about **4 km from seabed to mean sea level**;
- about **4 km above mean sea level**;
- about **8 km total vertical scale**;
- about **8 km × 8 km nominal base footprint**;
- tapered pyramid / truncated-pyramid / broad mountain geometry;
- source-material hypothesis: local seabed/rock only if future geological, environmental and legal work shows extraction is acceptable.

A perfect 8 km × 8 km pyramid with 8 km height has a geometric volume of about **171 km³**. At 2,700 kg/m³ representative rock density this is about **4.6 × 10^14 kg**, or roughly **460 billion tonnes**. A truncated summit and foundations would alter this substantially.

An “8 km crater” is therefore not a minor detail. The source volume, sediment thickness, excavation depth, crustal structure, slope stability and environmental disturbance must be modeled explicitly.

## Why investigate it?

The hypothesis is that a fixed full-depth landform could provide:

1. a permanent geodetic reference at 90°N;
2. vertically distributed sensors from seabed through ocean, ice and atmosphere;
3. repeatable comparison with satellite products and drifting platforms;
4. a controlled way to study how a large fixed obstacle changes currents, ice drift, mixing and atmosphere;
5. a long-term scientific facility for water-cycle, meteorology, air-quality and communications research.

The project must **not** frame construction as “build first and see what happens.” The correct sequence is measurement → simulation → independent review → scaled test → reversible demonstrator → decision.

## Existing near-pole measurements — correction to earlier wording

It is not correct to say that humanity has never made fixed measurements near the pole.

The **North Pole Environmental Observatory (NPEO)** maintained bottom-anchored oceanographic moorings on the Pole Abyssal Plain from 2001 to 2010, usually about 50–80 km from 90°N, in water about 4.3 km deep. Those systems measured current speed and direction, temperature, salinity, pressure and ice draft through the year. Since 2010 NPEO reports a bottom-pressure recorder on the seafloor at **90°N**.

What does not exist is the proposed **permanent land-like full-stack observatory exactly at 90°N** with a stable surface reference plus seabed, full-water-column, ice, atmospheric and communications instrumentation in one site.

## What current speeds are known?

There is no single defensible “one speed at the top and one speed at the bottom for every month” at 90°N. The current field changes with eddies, storms, ice state and depth.

### Upper ocean

Near-pole observations show background currents commonly of order **1–4 cm/s**. Aagaard et al. (2008), using a mooring about 55 km from the pole, reported eddy speeds typically **10–40 cm/s**, around ten times the background flow.

### Abyss / near bottom

Aagaard (1981) reported Arctic abyssal flow generally **below 1 cm/s**, with energetic episodes around **2–4 cm/s** and some deeper overflow events faster than that.

### Full-year modern record

AWI/PANGAEA mooring CAO2-01 in the Amundsen Basin covers August 2023–September 2024 and includes current meters around **215 m and 3,796 m**. As of August 2026 the raw archive remains under moratorium until October 2026, so monthly values must not be invented before the data are released.

A future 90°N station should publish monthly and daily statistics at many depths, including mean, median, percentiles, maxima, direction, turbulence, shear and separate ice-drift velocity.

## Could the island calm the ocean?

A full-depth 8 km-scale obstacle would almost certainly modify **local** circulation, but the sign of the effect cannot be assumed.

Potential effects include:

- flow blocking and diversion;
- acceleration around the flanks;
- wakes and recirculation;
- turbulence;
- upwelling/downwelling;
- internal waves;
- changed stratification and mixing;
- altered sea-ice stress and pressure-ridge formation;
- sediment scour and deposition.

Therefore the scientific question is:

> **How would an 8 km-scale full-depth obstacle redistribute kinetic energy, freshwater, heat and mixing in the central Arctic Ocean?**

A global “calming” effect is not established and should not be claimed without coupled ocean–ice–atmosphere simulations.

## Extreme weather and tornadoes

The ocean strongly influences weather and climate, and NOAA studies show links between large-scale ocean/atmosphere patterns and tornado probability.

But there is currently **no evidence that a single 90°N island would reduce tornadoes globally**. Tornadoes arise from atmospheric instability, moisture, vertical wind shear and storm-scale dynamics.

A 4 km-high Arctic mountain would, however, strongly disturb the **local atmosphere**. The project should test:

- orographic winds;
- mountain waves;
- cloud/precipitation formation;
- boundary-layer heat exchange;
- pressure-field changes;
- possible regional teleconnections.

If global severe-weather effects appear in ensembles, they must be shown to exceed natural variability before any conclusion is made.

## Sea ice — immediate mechanical challenge

Moving, deforming pack ice is likely a more direct engineering threat than the background ocean current.

A fixed island could cause:

- ice pile-up and large pressure ridges;
- accelerated ice flow around the sides;
- downstream leads/open-water zones;
- changed ocean-atmosphere heat flux;
- abrasion and impact loading;
- habitat changes.

These effects need coupled discrete-element/continuum sea-ice modeling plus physical tests.

## Tectonic plates

The idea that this mountain would control or “press” global tectonic plates is **not supported by current geophysics**. Plate motion is driven primarily by deep-Earth and gravity-related forces including mantle convection and slab pull, not by ocean currents.

However, moving roughly 10^14–10^15 kg of rock could create measurable **local** crustal loading/unloading, flexure and stress redistribution. Deep excavation could also interact with faults or unstable crust.

So the legitimate question is local:

> Could excavation and redistribution of hundreds of billions of tonnes of rock measurably deform or destabilize the crust near the construction site?

That must be solved with geotechnical, seismic and isostatic models before any excavation.

## Measurement-error statement

A 1 cm measurement error on Earth does **not** geometrically become hundreds of kilometres of error in orbit.

The real amplification problem is that centimetre-scale bias in sea-ice freeboard can become decimetre-scale error in derived ice thickness and then a large total volume/mass uncertainty when integrated over huge areas.

This project should therefore track uncertainty numerically rather than use dimensionally incorrect comparisons.

## Value for a 3D Earth model

A permanent 90°N site would not be required to build a valid 3D Earth model; existing geodesy, GNSS, altimetry, gravimetry, radar and lidar already provide high-precision global geometry.

Its special value would be as a **polar calibration and validation anchor** for ocean–ice–atmosphere observations in an environment whose surface is mobile and seasonal.

Possible instruments:

- geodetic GNSS monuments;
- absolute gravity;
- radar/laser calibration targets;
- seabed pressure and geodesy;
- ADCP/CTD chains from surface to bottom;
- ice draft sonar;
- meteorology, aerosol and radiation sensors;
- optical/thermal cameras;
- atmospheric profiling;
- satellite/aircraft calibration campaigns.

## Deep-ocean excavation machinery — concept only

If later studies ever justify using local seabed material, the project may study a new class of remotely operated deep-ocean construction systems:

- robotic seabed excavators;
- autonomous rock-cutting/dredging machines;
- low-plume sediment handling;
- subsea conveyors/lifting systems;
- geotechnical survey robots;
- continuous environmental monitoring.

This is a future R&D topic, not authorization to excavate the Arctic seabed.

## Open project position

The project may state the ambition clearly:

> **If high-resolution simulations, independent review and staged reversible Arctic demonstrations show that a permanent 90°N artificial island provides unique scientific value with acceptable environmental and geophysical risk, international construction should be considered.**

And equally clearly:

> **If the same scientific result is achievable with lower-impact fixed seabed instruments, moorings, mobile platforms or satellites, or if the environmental risk is unacceptable, the full-scale island should not be built.**

## Required research before any construction decision

1. exact bathymetry and crustal structure at 90°N;
2. source-material and excavation-volume balance;
3. ocean circulation with/without the island;
4. sea-ice drift and pressure-ridge modeling;
5. sediment plume/scour modeling;
6. geomechanical loading/unloading and induced-seismicity analysis;
7. atmospheric flow over a 4 km-high polar mountain;
8. regional/global climate ensemble tests;
9. ecosystem and biogeochemical impacts;
10. international legal and environmental review;
11. comparison with lower-impact observatory architectures.
